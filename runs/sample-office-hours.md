Facts about the place [AI judged]:

- Fewer than 10% of enrolled students attend OH in any given week.
- The department has ~400 undergraduates.
- OH happens in individual faculty offices on the department floor.
- Attendance spikes right before midterms/finals and is near zero otherwise.

4. It assumes the problem is attendance rather than what happens during the hours.
5. It assumes the department, not individual faculty, is the right unit to fix this.
6. It assumes students know office hours exist and what they are for.
7. It assumes faculty want more students to show up.
8. It assumes the barrier is on the student side, not the faculty or scheduling side.

## Stage 2 · /jtbd

### Step 1 — Place

### Step 2 — Six jobs
1. (Struggling, functional) When I have failed the last two problem sets and the midterm is in ten days, I want to sit with the professor and walk through one problem out loud, so I can see where my method actually breaks.
2. (Struggling, emotional) When I have been reading the textbook for three nights and still feel lost, I want to hear a professor say my confusion is normal at this point in the course, so I can stop believing I am the stupid one in the room.
3. (Shy, social) When I never speak in a lecture of 180 people, I want to be a recognizable face to the professor in a small quiet room, so I can later ask for a recommendation letter without feeling like a stranger.
4. (Top, social) When I already have the correct answer, I want to be seen by the professor as someone who thinks beyond the assigned problems, so I can be remembered when research assistant spots open up.
5. (Grad-school hopeful, functional) When I am deciding whether to apply to a PhD, I want to ask the professor what their week actually looks like, so I can judge whether I want that life.
6. (International, emotional) When the course norms and the slang in the group chat still feel foreign, I want ten unhurried minutes with a professor who will slow down, so I can feel I belong in this department and not just enrolled in it.

## Stage 3 · /hidden-assumption

### Step 1 — Place, role, old problem
University department (~400 undergrads). Faculty / department leadership. Old problem: how to get more students to office hours.

### Step 2 — Six whys (autopilot, insider answers [AI judged])
- Q1: Why "office hours" at all? / A1: Professors have research/teaching, can't be interrupted all day. "It's just how it is."
- Q2: Why only the professor? / A2: TAs run review but students treat professor time as the real thing because professors grade and write letters. "That's just how it is."
- Q3: What if help-giver != grader? / A3: Would feel weird; professor knows the exam. "That's just how we do it in a course."
- Q4: Why the professor's office? / A4: Books/computer/files; private room needed for sensitive conversations. "That's how academic buildings are set up."
- Q5: Why does getting help have to be private? / A5: Admitting you don't understand = exposing weakness. "We've always protected students' privacy about struggle."
- Q6: Why pull students toward the thing that signals weakness? / A6: 1:1 is where learning deepens; we want to give that gift. "Just how we were taught to think about it."

### Step 3 — Three-part sentence [AI judged]
People here assume one-on-one time with a professor is the gold-standard form of help, and that privacy is required because asking for help means exposing weakness.
I can see it in that we locate help in enclosed single-faculty offices on a work-week schedule, count attendance as success, and tolerate ~90% of students never showing up except before exams.
Nobody has checked it, because every faculty member went through this system themselves and found it formative; it feels like obviously-good pedagogy.

### Step 4 — HMW [AI judged]
How might we create help where being confused is visible and normal, so no student has to privately admit weakness in a professor's office?

### Step 5 — One-week test [AI judged]
Pick one course. For 5 weekdays, hold a 60-min "working session" in the department's shared lounge instead of in the professor's office. Tables open, anyone welcome; professor sits with laptop, works, answers any question raised at the table. Count distinct students present, questions asked out loud. Wrong if fewer than 20 distinct students across the week, OR fewer than 1 out-loud question per session.

Critic: all three questions pass (counts behavior, "wrong" named, reproducible).

## Stage 4 · /triz

### Step 1 — Problem + first idea
Problem: Stage 3 HMW. First idea [AI judged]: move OH into the shared lounge, open table, drop-in.

### Step 2 — Objector
Tenured professor: the lounge is where students eat/flirt/vent; the quiet or failing students will walk past because being seen asking is more exposing than knocking on a closed door, and only the confident will come.
If we improve [visibility and social normalness of asking for help], then [the privacy that lets a struggling or shy student approach a professor at all] gets worse.

### Step 3 — P and not P [AI judged]
The setting where help happens must be visible to peers, so that asking looks normal and shame drops, and not visible to peers, so that struggling or shy students aren't exposed to the loud/confident crowd.

### Step 4 — Four separations
- Time: Yes — "attendance spikes before midterms/finals" (visible-help need is bursty; shy concern is constant).
- Place: Yes — the lounge vs individual offices on the same floor.
- Case: Yes — shy/failing students vs confident ones already using DMs and group chats.
- Parts/whole: Yes — attendance <10%/week looks absent, but individuals ARE asking in DMs/chats/TAs.

Picked separation: case. Picked principle: 1 Segmentation.

### Step 5 — Ideas
1. Two LMS help channels: public "Open Help" + private "Quiet Help" (TA-only). Worse off: strong browsers lose quiet-room material.
2. Three-way toggle on every Ask button (Post as Me / Anonymous / Send to TA only). Worse off: TAs flooded with one-to-one anon messages.
3. Route by midterm score into "coaching pod" vs class chat. Worse off: borderline students labeled "the failing group".

Picked: Idea 1 — preserves normalization (public default stays visible) AND gives shy students a side door.

### Final test [AI judged]
In one course, run both channels for 5 weekdays. Count (a) distinct Open posters, (b) distinct Quiet users, (c) total help-seekers. Wrong if total distinct help-seekers <3× typical OH baseline, OR fewer than 3 distinct Quiet users (meaning segmentation was overbuilt).
